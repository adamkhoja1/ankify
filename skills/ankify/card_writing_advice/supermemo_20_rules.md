# Twenty Rules of Formulating Knowledge

Source: https://www.supermemo.com/en/blog/twenty-rules-of-formulating-knowledge
Author: Piotr Woźniak (creator of SuperMemo)

This is the foundational document for flashcard creation, widely referenced in the spaced repetition community.

---

## 1. Do Not Learn If You Do Not Understand

Comprehension must precede memorization. Learning without understanding produces negligible knowledge value. Build foundational understanding before attempting to memorize specific details.

## 2. Learn Before You Memorize

"Build an overall picture of the learned knowledge" before memorizing individual facts. Read complete chapters establishing context before breaking material into discrete Q&A pairs for spaced repetition.

## 3. Build Upon the Basics

Start with simple foundational models rather than complete detail. Basic knowledge is easier to retain and takes minimal repetition time. "Better err on the safe side" with elementary concepts.

## 4. Stick to the Minimum Information Principle

Formulate material as simply as possible. Simple items are easier to remember because the brain processes them consistently. Split complex items into multiple sub-items to allow individual pacing of repetitions.

## 5. Cloze Deletion Is Easy and Effective

Use sentences with missing words/phrases replaced by dots: "Bill …[name] was the second US president." This technique rapidly converts textbook knowledge into spaced-repetition-compatible material.

## 6. Use Imagery

Visual cortex processes images far more effectively than verbal information. Graphics dramatically reduce learning time for anatomy, geography, geometry, and chemistry. "One picture is worth a thousand words."

## 7. Use Mnemonic Techniques

Employ mind maps, peg lists, and memory palace methods. These techniques make memorization easier, though they're only needed for 1-5% of items. Master basic techniques through practice and resources by Tony Buzan.

## 8. Graphic Deletion Is As Good As Cloze Deletion

Cover portions of illustrations and ask students to name missing areas. Single complex images can generate 10-20 items. Highly effective for anatomy and geography learning.

## 9. Avoid Sets

"Avoid sets!" Collections without order (like listing EU members randomly) are nearly impossible to memorize. Convert unordered sets into enumerations with logical sequencing to force consistent brain processing.

## 10. Avoid Enumerations

Ordered lists are still difficult but preferable to sets. Use overlapping cloze deletions to break enumerations into smaller chunks: instead of the full alphabet, practice "A…E" then "B…F" sequences.

## 11. Combat Interference

Similar items cause confusion and forgetting. Use vivid examples, context cues, emotional associations, and personal references to distinguish between confusable material. Detect and eliminate interference promptly.

## 12. Optimize Wording

Reduce complex sentences into concise, specific questions. Eliminate redundant context. "The shorter the initial chapter...the better." Focus on essential information to retrieve, making answers as brief as possible.

## 13. Refer to Other Memories

Build new knowledge upon established memories. Using words like "humble" and "supplicant" to clarify "cringing" leverages existing knowledge and reduces interference through semantic connections.

## 14. Personalize and Provide Examples

Connect knowledge to personal experience. "Personalized examples are very resistant to interference." Reference people, places, and events from your life to strengthen retention and reduce learning time.

## 15. Rely on Emotional States

Associate material with vivid or emotionally charged examples. Reference emotionally significant people, events, or scenarios. "Emotions are related to memories"—leveraging emotional connections dramatically improves recall.

## 16. Context Cues Simplify Wording

Use category labels, different templates for knowledge branches, and reference tags to provide context. "bioch: GRE" eliminates wording about biochemistry by establishing context upfront.

## 17. Redundancy Does Not Contradict Minimum Information Principle

Multiple valid representations of knowledge are beneficial. Active/passive word pairs, reasoning steps, derivation sequences, and different semantic angles are welcome redundancy that strengthens memories.

## 18. Provide Sources

Include source attribution for non-obvious knowledge. Sources enable verification, reliability judgment, knowledge updates, and real-world defense of your knowledge. Exclude source information from learned content itself.

## 19. Provide Date Stamping

Tag volatile knowledge with collection dates or version numbers (statistical figures, software versions, economic indicators). Update items as information changes. Date stamps assist verification without requiring memorization of dates.

## 20. Prioritize

Effective learning requires constant prioritization: select quality sources, extract essential knowledge, determine transfer timing, formulate carefully, and manage learning priorities actively.
